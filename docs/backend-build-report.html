01What was built

A complete scanning engine: roughly 4,200 lines of TypeScript across 45 source files, with a PostgreSQL database behind it. It is one application that can be used three different ways.

  • A worker that performs a full scan, from searching to writing the report.
  • A scheduler that triggers the worker every morning without anyone involved.
  • An API that serves the stored results to the dashboard, read-only, so it can never accidentally spend money.

Each nightly run searches the configured locations, converts every offer into a comparable monthly price, stores it, compares it against yesterday, and produces a report of the cheapest options with what has changed.

What it does with each offer

Three pieces of logic do the real work, and all three are covered by tests.

  • Vehicle identification. Decides whether something is a minivan, a 12-passenger van or a large SUV, using the industry vehicle code where available and otherwise seat counts and a maintained list of model names. It records why each decision was made, so a wrong answer can be traced rather than guessed at.
  • Deal identity. Gives every deal a stable identity from location, supplier, vehicle, pickup date and length. Without it, the same car would look like a brand new discovery every morning.
  • Change detection. Marks deals as new, cheaper, dearer or gone, ignoring movements below 3% or $20 so ordinary daily noise stays out of the report.

02What today's live runs showed

Five scans ran against Atlanta. The first working scan looked successful but was quietly returning the wrong thing, and fixing that took three attempts.

  • The first scan found only peer-to-peer listings. Six qualifying deals, all from Turo, which your brief excludes and which cannot be extended into a monthly programme.
  • The cause was our own search shape. The comparison site ranks everything by price, so asking for vans and SUVs together spent the whole budget on cheap compact SUVs. The vans were never reached.
  • Splitting the search per vehicle type fixed it. 12-passenger vans went from zero results to 38, and Enterprise, Hertz, Avis, Budget, Dollar and Thrifty all appeared.

A correction to an earlier verbal update

We reported that the major rental companies were absent from the data. That was wrong. They were there the whole time, hidden behind a result limit. An uncapped test returned 425 offers across 11 suppliers.

Three data bugs found and fixed

Inspecting the raw supplier data revealed that three fields were being read from the wrong place and silently discarded: the industry vehicle code, the seat count, and the fuel policy. All three are fixed, and six tests built from a real supplier response now guard them.

One consequence matters commercially. A vehicle we previously reported as seating 12 is actually reported by the source as seating 8; the figure 12 came from our own fallback filling a gap. Seat counts now come from the source, but any 12-passenger candidate should still be confirmed on the supplier's own website before booking.

04The decision we need: which locations?

The scanner works. What it does not know is where to look. The brief said “throughout the United States”, and that phrase can mean two very different systems with very different costs.

A · Everywhere in the US

The literal reading of the brief

  • Every major airport and city branch nationwide
  • Finds bargains anywhere, including places you would never think to check
  • Enterprise alone has around 4,400 US branches, so “everywhere” has to be capped somewhere regardless
  • At 250 locations this costs roughly $3,500 a month

B · A list you choose

What the system is built for today

  • You name the airports or cities that matter to you
  • We scan exactly those every morning
  • Adding or removing a location is a one-line change, no development
  • Ten locations costs roughly $140 a month

The cost is driven by how many searches run each day, and every location multiplies it. These figures are measured, not estimated: each location costs about $0.47 per pickup date scanned.

Genuine nationwide coverage is not affordable on this data source

The original plan estimated $35 to $60 a month. Real pricing puts even a 250-location daily sweep at around $3,500 a month, and that is still far short of “every location in the US”. Wide coverage would need an official partner agreement with a rental aggregator, which is cheaper per search and contractually clean, but requires a qualifying business and an application process.

Today's account would also need upgrading

The current data-provider plan allows four searches at once and includes $5 of credit a month, of which $1.74 is already used. Anything beyond a handful of locations needs a paid plan before it can run at all.

05Questions for you

The first three shape the daily job directly. The last two we have asked before and still need answered.

Question 01

All US locations, or a list you choose?

  • A list you choose. Tell us the airports or cities and we scan those every morning.
  • As wide as the budget allows. Give us a monthly figure and we fit the largest sensible list inside it.
  • Genuinely nationwide. Requires an official partner agreement first; not possible on the current source at sane cost.
Our suggestion Start with a list of 5 to 10, chosen for where you would actually collect a vehicle. After two weeks of real prices we will know whether wider coverage is worth paying for, instead of guessing now.
Question 02

Which locations, specifically?

Ten major airports are configured as a starting point: Los Angeles, San Francisco, Las Vegas, Phoenix, Dallas, Denver, Chicago, Atlanta, Orlando and New York. Only Atlanta has actually been scanned.

Airports usually hold the largest fleets, but city branches are often cheaper for long rentals because they avoid airport surcharges. We can scan either.

What we need A list of places, or confirmation that the ten above are right. If there is a region you would actually drive from, say so; scanning cities you cannot reach is wasted money.
Question 03

How many pickup dates, and how often should it run?

Today it checks three pickup dates per location, at 3, 14 and 28 days ahead, which triples the cost. Prices for monthly rentals move slowly, so daily checking of three separate dates may be more than you need.

  • One pickup date, checked daily — cheapest, still spots price drops
  • Three pickup dates, checked daily — three times the cost, better coverage of the booking window
  • Three pickup dates, checked twice a week — a middle path
Our suggestion One pickup date daily to begin with. Daily checking is what makes price-drop alerts possible, so we would rather cut the number of dates than the frequency.
Question 04

Is $1,100 a month still the target?

Nothing at Atlanta met it. We can keep the target and accept quiet days, raise it to something the market offers, or set a different figure for each vehicle type.

Our suggestion Decide after two weeks of real prices from your chosen locations.
Question 05

Has anyone confirmed the long-term extension with a supplier?

No booking site quotes a single 330-day rental, so the scanner prices a 30-day rental and notes which monthly programme each supplier runs. Nobody has yet phoned Enterprise or Avis to confirm that a booking really extends toward eleven months, or what it costs when it does.

What we need One phone call to one branch about one vehicle. Until that happens, every monthly figure we report is a 30-day price rather than a proven long-term rate.

06What happens once you answer

Question 1 to 3 are the only things standing between the current state and a scanner that runs itself every morning. With those answered:

  • The location list goes into configuration, which takes minutes rather than development work.
  • The daily job gets a supervisor so it survives restarts and runs unattended.
  • Reports start arriving by email or Slack, once you give us an address.
  • After two consecutive runs, price-change alerts begin working, since there is finally a yesterday to compare against.

Everything else on the backend is finished and tested.
